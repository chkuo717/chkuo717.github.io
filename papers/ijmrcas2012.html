Eight ways to pivot about the incision

A surgical instrument that passes through a small incision can only tilt two ways about it, spin, and slide in and out. This overview turns that constraint into design goals for the kinematics of a surgical robot, and sorts the mechanisms that build the pivot into their own structure, remote-centre-of-motion mechanisms, into eight types.

What you are seeing

A remote centre of motion is a fixed point about which a link of a mechanism rotates, located away from the mechanism itself, outside the space its other links sweep. Put that point at the incision and the instrument can only pivot about it, whatever the motors do: the constraint is held by the structure, not by the controller. Each sketch keeps its red point still while the instrument swings.

Six of the types place the point away from the hardware. The isocenter and the gimbal put a joint at the point itself; they keep the pivot, but the paper's comparison marks them as lacking the extracorporeal workspace the others offer. Only the parallel wrist can, in principle, drive all three rotations independently.

Coupled and decoupled tilts

The idea

The overview starts from the motion an incision allows: two rotations about the entry point, a spin about the instrument's axis and a translation along it, four degrees of freedom, with every moving part of the robot kept outside the patient. From the surgical concerns of safety, accuracy, ergonomics and dexterity it derives eight design goals, six design requirements and four design preferences for the kinematics, and ties each to a mechanism design issue: pivoting motion, decoupled motion, workspace, isotropy, backdrivability and redundancy.

Decoupling gets a classification of its own. Writing the instrument's motion as the two tilts about the entry point, the spin and the insertion, five types follow from which of these each actuator controls alone: the translation only (Type I), some rotations (II), some rotations and the translation (III), all rotations (IV), and all four (V). Type III was the most common in the robots then in use: a double parallelogram, for instance, tilts the instrument about two axes in series, so its second tilt depends on its first actuator as well, and the spin and insertion are added by two more actuators carried on the output link.

In the paper

The eight types are compared for the rotations they give without extra actuators, the decoupling they allow, their working space outside the patient, isotropy and backdrivability, and 39 robots are listed with the kind of remote centre they use. Two problems are left open: a fully decoupled 4-DOF parallel mechanism with three rotations and one translation about a remote centre, and a fully isotropic one. A fully decoupled design was published by Kuo and Dai the same year (animation).

The sketches show each type's principle in the plane or in an isometric view; they are not drawings of particular robots.

The paper. Kuo, C.-H., Dai, J. S. and Dasgupta, P., "Kinematic design considerations for minimally invasive surgical robots: an overview," International Journal of Medical Robotics and Computer Assisted Surgery, 2012. doi:10.1002/rcs.453
