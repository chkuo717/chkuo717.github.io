A Halbach cylinder that re-sets to the load

Eight magnets in a ring make a uniform field; a magnet at the centre, fixed to an arm, is pushed round by that field just as hard as gravity pulls the arm down. Turn one lever and the ring's field weakens evenly, so the same device balances a lighter load.

What you are seeing

The eight outer magnets are a Halbach cylinder: each is magnetised at twice its angle round the ring, an arrangement that adds the fields of all eight into one uniform field across the bore and almost nothing outside. The rotor magnet at the centre sits in that field with its north pole pointing along the arm. A magnet in a uniform field feels a torque proportional to the sine of the angle between them, which is exactly how the torque of a weight on an arm varies with the arm's angle. Match the two amplitudes and the arm is balanced at every angle, with nothing touching.

Each outer magnet is embedded in a spur gear, and the eight gears mesh in a ring, so neighbouring gears turn opposite ways. Turning the lever on one gear therefore turns the magnets alternately by +ϕ and −ϕ. That particular motion keeps the field's direction and uniformity and scales its strength by cos ϕ: from full at ϕ = 0 to nothing at ϕ = 90°. The lever is the payload adjustment.

The idea

A balancer tuned to one load is of limited use. Spring balancers are re-tuned by moving spring ends or changing stiffness, which needs extra mechanism and usually some work against the spring. Here the whole adjustment is one lever that turns a one-degree-of-freedom gear train. The paper's design condition is simply ϕ = cos−1(mPgL/nBmax): any payload up to the capacity nBmax/gL has a lever angle that balances it. A pleasant consequence, found in the tests, is that re-setting costs almost no work when the arm is held horizontal: the torque on the lever remains approximately zero near that position, so turning it from on to off and back is nearly energy-free there.

In the paper

Finite-element simulation of the eight-magnet cylinder with a cubic rotor magnet gave a peak torque of 1.67 N m at full field, falling smoothly to almost nothing at ϕ = 90°; the built prototype measured 1.72 N m, with the measured curve a close sinusoid. Torques on the lever were measured too, and the work of a full re-set computed for each arm angle: up to 1.36 J to be supplied with the arm up, 1.64 J released with the arm down, and close to zero with the arm horizontal. The prototype and the torque map are pictured on the Magnetic mechanisms page.

The animation uses the paper's model: torque nBmax cos ϕ sin θ against mPgL sin θ, with a small added ripple to stand for the discreteness of a real ring. Dimensions are illustrative; the lever and gear train are drawn schematically.
