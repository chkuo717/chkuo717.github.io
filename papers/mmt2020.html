The gear-spring module

A gear pair, a short rod and a compression spring, all packed along a robot's arm, take most of the arm's weight off its motor. Three of them balance a Delta robot without getting in the way of its workspace.

What you are seeing

Everything is mounted on the rotating link, which is what makes the module compact: there is no spring anchored to a wall behind the robot and no extra link reaching back to the ground. Gear 1 is fixed to the pivot. Gear 2, twice its size, is carried on the link and meshes with it, so as the link turns through an angle θ gear 2 turns through θ/2 relative to the link. A crank pin on gear 2 drives a slider along the link through a short connecting rod, and the slider compresses a spring that is at its free length when the link points straight up.

The graph shows the torque at the pivot. Gravity's torque is a sine wave; the spring's torque is very nearly its mirror image, so the net torque, which the motor would have to supply, is small. It is not exactly zero. That is honest: the module is an approximation, and the readout under the figure gives the leftover as a share of the unbalanced peak.

The idea

With a long connecting rod the slider travel is close to r sin(θ/2), where r is the crank radius, and the spring energy close to ¼ k r² (1 − cos θ), the same shape as the weight's energy mgs cos θ. Choosing k = 4mgs/r² cancels the two; a gear ratio of 2 and a crank set at right angles to the link at the start are what make the match possible. The shorter the rod, the more the slider's motion departs from the sine and the larger the leftover, which the rod-length slider lets you see directly. The paper's second route accepts the rod as built and chooses the stiffness by optimisation so that the peak motor torque is as small as possible; that is what the optimisation tick-box does here, and it brings the leftover down to a few per cent even with a short rod.

In the paper

One module is fitted to each of the three upper arms of a Delta robot, and the dimensions are chosen so that the balance is exact at a chosen set of configurations and close elsewhere. On a theoretical model the motor torques were reduced over 86 % of the workspace; applied to the dimensions of an industrial Delta robot carrying a 10 kgf platform through a pick-and-place cycle, the peak torque and the energy consumed fell by up to 38.4 % and 55.4 %. The module itself was introduced for planar robot arms in a companion paper, Nguyen, Lin and Kuo (2020) in the ASME Journal of Mechanisms and Robotics, where a one-link prototype gave a power reduction of 86.5 % in tests.

The animation shows one module on a single link, with the paper's design rules and illustrative dimensions. The torque curves are computed from the full slider-crank geometry, so the leftover you see is the real effect of a finite rod.

The paper. Nguyen, V. L., Lin, C.-Y. and Kuo, C.-H., "Gravity compensation design of Delta parallel robots using gear-spring modules," Mechanism and Machine Theory, 2020. doi:10.1016/j.mechmachtheory.2020.104046
