Floating a load on two magnets

One small magnet, carrying two hundred times its own weight, floats above one larger magnet, and keeps floating as it moves up and down through three times its own height. The trick is in the shape cut into the top of the larger magnet.

What you are seeing

A cross-section through the two magnets. The stator is a 30 mm cube of N38 neodymium with a trench cut across its top; the mover is a 5 mm cube of N50, on a rod that passes through a cap on the stator and carries the payload. Both are magnetised upwards, so the mover is above two like poles and is pushed up. Near a flat pole face that push would fall off quickly with height. In the trench the two sloping faces close in as the mover descends and open out as it rises, and the field gradient along the axis, which is what the force depends on, becomes nearly constant. The mover therefore feels about the same lift at every height in the 15 mm stroke, and a 2 N load, two hundred times the mover's weight, rides along with it.

The idea

Previous passive levitators used many magnets, or held their load at one height only. Here there are two magnets and a long stroke, and the design variable is the shape of the cavity. The paper writes the stator's profile as a function, either a parabola or a combination of straight segments, and searches for the coefficients that make the force on the mover match the load over the stroke, computing the force at each trial with the authors' Monte Carlo method for arbitrary magnet shapes (the animation of that method). The V-shaped profile, a flat floor with straight sloping sides, comes out far better than the parabola: an average gravity reduction of 93 % against 65 %, because the straight slopes produce a more nearly linear field along the axis.

In the paper

The optimised V profile was machined into a real magnet and tested on a prototype with a 3D-printed cap and slider; the measured average gravity reduction was 86 %, with the gap to the computed 93 % traced to the actual magnetisation of the stock magnets and small misalignments. The paper then explores the design space: cubic, cylindrical and spherical movers give force curves of the same shape and strengths proportional to their volume; a mover-to-stator side ratio of about 1 : 3 gives the best force for the range; and the design scales up, with a 75 mm stator carrying 250 N, at a higher force-to-volume ratio than earlier levitators.

Geometry and magnetisations are the paper's (Tables I to III). The force in the animation is computed from a surface-charge model with the mover as 27 dipoles; it reproduces the paper's curves and gravity-reduction figures to within a few per cent.

The paper. Zeng, X., Li, S., Kuo, C.-H. and Sariyildiz, E., "Passive levitation in long vertical range using two permanent magnets," IEEE/ASME Transactions on Mechatronics, 2026. doi:10.1109/TMECH.2025.3629126
