About

Chin-Hsing Kuo

Mechanical engineer. I work on mechanisms and robots, and in particular on how springs, weights and magnets can be arranged so that a machine carries itself.

I am a Senior Lecturer in the School of Engineering at the University of Wollongong, Australia, where I also serve as Academic Program Director for Mechanical Engineering. Before moving to Wollongong in 2019 I spent eight years on the faculty of the National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, as Assistant Professor and then tenured Associate Professor. In 2017 I taught a course on static balancing of mechanisms and robots at the Tokyo Institute of Technology as a specially appointed associate professor, and I returned there in 2023–24 as a JSPS International Research Fellow to work on gravity-compensated assistive exoskeletons. I have also been a visiting scholar at Delft University of Technology (2014) and a visiting research professor at the Medical Device Center of the University of Minnesota (2012). I obtained my PhD from King's College London in 2011.

My research is organised around mechanical intelligence: the capacity of mechanisms and robots to adapt and to perform through their inherent mechanics, with minimal added sensing, computation, control and actuation. I am best known for work on static balancing and gravity compensation, one of its founding classes. The programme also covers magnetic mechanisms, from contactless springs to gravity compensators and levitators; mechanism theory and robot kinematics, including reconfigurable mechanisms, decoupled parallel manipulators and grippers; medical and assistive devices; mechanisms for energy harvesting; and the history of mechanisms and mechanical computation. I have published more than a hundred peer-reviewed papers and hold four granted US patents, two of which have been transferred to industry. Thirty-five research degrees have been completed under my principal supervision, and former students and postdoctoral researchers now hold faculty and postdoctoral positions in the United States, Vietnam and Taiwan.

Beyond the lab

I keep a collection of some forty planimeters, the mechanical instruments that measure area by tracing a boundary. Instruments from it have been lent to two exhibitions at the National Science and Technology Museum in Kaohsiung, Taiwan (2024–25 and 2026), and I gave a public lecture on their history and mechanisms in 2026 (Talks & Teaching). A site about the collection, in Chinese and English, is in preparation.
