Where a moving body turns about

Move a rigid body from one place to another in the plane, however you like, and there is always one point about which the whole move is a single rotation. Finding that point is the first construction of graphical kinematics, and most of linkage synthesis is built on it.

What you are seeing

The body is shown in its first and second positions. Each point of it, A and B, has moved along a chord; the perpendicular bisector of a chord is the set of points equidistant from its ends, so a centre of rotation that takes A1 to A2 must lie on one bisector, and one that takes B1 to B2 on the other. Their intersection is the only point that does both: the relative pole P12. The moving copy confirms it, sweeping from position 1 to position 2 along arcs centred on the pole.

The idea

The pole turns problems about motion into problems about points and lines. To design a four-bar linkage that carries a body through two given positions, the fixed pivots can be anywhere on the bisectors, which is the classical two-position synthesis; three and four positions follow with further poles. The same point serves force analysis: in a mechanism, the instant centre between two links is the relative pole of their motion, and the lines joining instant centres give force directions without writing equations. The e-book develops both uses with constructions the reader can drag.

In the paper

The e-book, built in GeoGebra, is used in Kuo's teaching of kinematics of machinery; each page is a live construction where points can be moved and the consequences watched, from the relative pole through two- and three-position synthesis to force analysis by instant centres. It is open at geogebra.org.
