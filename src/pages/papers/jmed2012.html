Four motions about one point, one motor each

A surgical tool through a small incision may tilt two ways, spin, and slide in and out, always pivoting about the incision. This manipulator gives each of those four motions its own motor, so that commanding one changes nothing else.

What you are seeing

The red dot is the remote centre of motion, the point on the abdominal wall where the tool enters. The tool's direction w is described by two projective angles, the angles of its shadows on the xz- and yz-planes; its spin about its own axis and its depth of insertion complete the four degrees of freedom a surgical instrument has at an incision. In the manipulator each of these four is driven by one actuator: moving a slider here does what moving that actuator does, and the other three readings do not change.

The idea

Earlier remote-centre-of-motion mechanisms for surgery coupled their motions, so a simple command such as tilting the tool in one plane needed several motors moving together under computer control. The paper first sets out the projective displacement representation for this special kinematics, then builds the manipulator from a motion constraint leg, an RRRP chain whose three revolute axes meet at the incision point, which alone gives the end-effector exactly the 3R1T remote-centre motion, and two auxiliary RRRUR legs that add no constraint. The actuated joints are the base R-joints of the two auxiliary legs and the P-joint and its adjacent R-joint in the motion constraint leg (the motion constraint generator method). The geometry is arranged so that the Jacobian is the identity: fully decoupled kinematics, which follows from the geometry of the legs.

In the paper

The inverse kinematics follows from the decoupling, the Jacobian and singularities are obtained by the screw system approach, and the reachable and collision-free workspaces are analysed. A CAD model and an installation sketch show the manipulator over a patient's bed. The approach grew into the general theory of decoupled projective motion.

Only the output motion is animated; the leg structure is described in the text and drawn in the paper's Fig. 4.
