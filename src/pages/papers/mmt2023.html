Perfect balancing with a Cardan gear

A small gear turning inside one twice its size makes a point move in a straight line. Lay an ordinary spring along that line and it balances a swinging arm exactly, at every angle.

What you are seeing

The arm is fixed to a small gear that turns about the ground pivot. Around it, slightly below, is a ring gear twice its diameter, also pivoted to the ground and free to turn. Because the small gear meshes inside the ring, the ring turns through half the arm's angle. The point A, marked in red on the small gear's pitch circle, traces a straight line on the ring (the dashed line PQ): it is the Cardan straight-line motion, familiar from drawing instruments, turned inside out so that the line itself rotates with the ring.

Two compression springs sit on that line, one seated at each end. As the arm swings one way, A moves towards Q and compresses that spring; the other spring is left at its free length, touching nothing. Swing the other way and they exchange roles. The graph shows that the energy stored in the springs rises exactly as the energy of the weight falls, so the sum is a flat line and the arm rests at any angle. Switch the springs off and the weight takes over.

The idea

The usual spring balancer needs a zero-free-length spring, a spring whose force is proportional to its full length rather than to its stretch. Real springs are not like that, so most designs add pulleys, cables or extra links to imitate one. The Cardan gear does it with geometry instead. A point on the small gear sits a distance 2r sin(θ/2) from the centre of the ring, where r is the small gear's pitch radius and θ the arm's angle. The square of that distance is proportional to 1 − cos θ, which is precisely how the height of the weight varies. So an ordinary spring of stiffness k = mgL/r², at its free length when the arm is upright, stores exactly the energy that gravity gives up, whatever its free length happens to be. No pre-tension, no cable, no auxiliary linkage.

In the paper

The paper derives this condition with the masses of all the links included, and works through the practical choices: compression springs lose contact with A for half of each turn, so two of them, one on each side, give unlimited rotation (the design animated here); an extension spring carries an initial tension that spoils the balance, which a small counterweight on the ring gear restores. Several variants follow, the balancer is applied to two multi-degree-of-freedom manipulators, and a prototype of the one-degree-of-freedom design is built and tested for the friction it adds. The photograph on the Gravity balancing page shows that prototype holding its arm at different angles.
