A magnet array for a long vertical stroke

A slider carrying twenty times its magnet's weight glides up and down sixty millimetres, held up by nothing but the magnets fixed to the frame around it. Their positions and tilts were found by optimisation, not by trial.

What you are seeing

The mover is a 10 mm cube magnetised upwards, fixed to a slider that runs vertically through the frame. Around it, in four columns symmetric about the two vertical planes, sit twenty identical cubes, five per column at 15 mm spacing. Each stator cube is magnetised along one of its own edges and is mounted with a small pitch and a yaw of about half a turn (the paper's Table 3), so the magnetisations are tilted a little up or down. Their combined pull and push on the mover adds to a lifting force that stays close to the 1.5 N load, twenty times the mover's weight, as the slider travels ±30 mm, six times the mover's height.

The idea

The force between two magnets changes steeply with distance, so a single stator magnet cannot hold a mover over a long stroke. An array can: each stator magnet contributes most over a short band of heights, and if the bands are arranged well the sum is flat. Finding that arrangement by hand, with hours of finite-element simulation per trial, had given a usable design before. This paper replaces it with an optimisation: the twenty design variables are the positions and the two tilt angles of the five magnets in a column, the force at each height is computed with the authors' Monte Carlo integration of the magnet-force integral (animated here), and a genetic algorithm searches for the arrangement that minimises the average gap between force and load. The result improves the earlier design from 86 % to 92 % average gravity reduction, and takes half an hour, where one finite-element run had taken about 8 h.

In the paper

A second example stacks three magnets on the mover to lift a heavier load, with the stator columns re-optimised for stock N38 magnets, and that design was built and tested; the prototype compensated 86.31 % of the 3.5 N payload against 90.49 % computed, without the small oscillations of the computed curve. The paper then studies the design space: how many stator magnets a column needs, the best ratio of magnet size to travel, how to amplify the capacity, and how the whole array scales. The prototype and its force curve are on the Magnetic mechanisms page.

The positions and angles are the paper's Table 3 (Example I); the force in the animation comes from a surface-charge model of the same dipole physics, with the mover as 27 dipoles, and comes out a little flatter than the paper's own Monte Carlo result (the message under the figure gives both figures).
