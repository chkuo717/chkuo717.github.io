The force between any two magnets, by random sampling

Throw random points into two magnets, add up the pull between each pair of points that land inside, and the total settles on the force between the magnets, whatever their shapes. That is the method behind the magnetic designs on this site.

What you are seeing

Two magnets, drawn in section, each enclosed in a dashed box. Every frame, points are placed at random in the two boxes. If a point falls inside magnet I and its partner inside magnet J, the pair contributes the force between two tiny magnetic dipoles at those positions; if either falls outside, the pair contributes nothing. The running average of those contributions, multiplied by the volumes of the boxes, is the estimate of the force, and the graph shows it settling on the value found with a very large number of samples. The coloured dots are the pairs that counted.

The idea

The force between two magnets can be written exactly as a sum over every pair of volume elements, one in each magnet, of the dipole force between them: a six-dimensional integral. For a few tidy cases, parallel cubes or coaxial cylinders, that integral has been reduced by hand to formulas; change the shape, tilt one magnet, or magnetise it along a different axis and the formula no longer applies. The paper does two things. It writes the integral once, in a form whose bounds are simply the two magnets' shapes and positions, so that any pair of magnets is described by the same equation. And it evaluates that integral by Monte Carlo sampling, whose cost does not grow with the number of dimensions and whose only input is a rule for deciding whether a point is inside a magnet. The accuracy against finite-element analysis is better than 98 % in the paper's examples, at a small fraction of the computing time.

The same property makes the method a design tool. If the shape of a magnet is unknown and the required force is given, the shape parameters become the variables of an optimisation, the integral is re-evaluated at each trial, and no formula has to be re-derived. The paper's last example shapes a magnet with a parabolic trench so that the force on a small cube above it falls linearly with height; the error against the target drops from 100 % to 5 %. That is the approach behind the two-magnet levitator and the long-stroke magnet array.

In the paper

Four analysis examples cover magnets of different shapes, non-aligned magnetisations and non-parallel positions, compared with finite-element results; a fifth is the synthesis example above. The discussion weighs accuracy against the number of samples, compares random with low-discrepancy sampling sequences, and sets the method against finite-element analysis on cost. The paper is the computational foundation of the group's later magnetic designs.

The animation runs the paper's estimator for Example 1 in your browser, with the paper's geometry and magnetisation. The reference value is a 60 000-sample run made when the page loads or the height changes; the force is attractive, so it is negative.
